An Indian offer letter usually quotes one number. Your bank statement shows a different one. Between them sit two more figures that appear on your payslip, and the four are routinely used as if they were interchangeable.

They are not. Here is what each actually contains.

## The four numbers

**CTC (Cost to Company)** — everything your employer spends on employing you in a year. Your salary, plus the employer's Provident Fund and ESI contributions, plus gratuity provision, plus insurance premiums and any other benefits with a rupee value attached.

**Gross salary** — your total earnings for a month before any of your own deductions. Basic, House Rent Allowance, allowances, and any variable pay for that month.

**Net salary** — gross minus your deductions: employee PF, employee ESI, Professional Tax and TDS.

**In-hand salary** — what is actually credited. In an ordinary month this equals net salary.

The order is fixed, and each step only ever subtracts:

> CTC → *(remove employer contributions and non-cash components)* → **Gross** → *(remove employee deductions)* → **Net** → *(adjust one-offs)* → **In-hand**

## Why CTC is bigger than gross

This is where most of the confusion sits, and the answer is not "deductions." Two different things separate CTC from gross.

**Employer contributions.** The employer's 12% Provident Fund contribution and 3.25% ESI contribution are genuine costs, and they are paid on your behalf — but to the government, not to you. They appear in CTC because the company spends them. They never appear in gross because they were never your earnings. The [PF calculator](/resources/pf-calculator) shows both sides of this split.

**Non-cash components.** Gratuity provision, insurance premiums, and sometimes meal or transport benefits sit in CTC without becoming monthly cash. Gratuity in particular is money you may receive years later, subject to eligibility conditions — counting it as current salary overstates what you have to spend.

Neither of these is a deduction from your pay. They are amounts that were never payable to you directly in the first place, which is why "CTC minus deductions equals take-home" produces the wrong answer.

## Why gross is bigger than net

This part *is* deductions, and there are four:

| Deduction | Calculated on | Applies when |
|---|---|---|
| Employee PF | Basic + DA | Generally 12%, subject to the wage ceiling |
| Employee ESI | Gross | Only where gross is within the ESI ceiling |
| Professional Tax | Gross, by slab | In states that levy it |
| TDS | Projected annual income | Where annual income is taxable |

Only PF and ESI have a fixed relationship to your salary. [Professional Tax](/resources/professional-tax-guide) depends on the state you work in, and some states do not levy it at all. [TDS](/blog/tds-on-salary) depends on your tax regime, your declared investments and your total income — which is why two people with identical gross salaries can have very different net pay.

## When net and in-hand differ

Most months they do not. The exceptions are one-off adjustments that sit outside the salary calculation:

- **Reimbursements** paid alongside salary — these push in-hand *above* net, because they are repayments of money you already spent rather than earnings
- **Notice-period recovery** during an exit
- **Salary advances** being adjusted
- **Loan or asset recoveries** agreed with the employer

If your credit does not match the net pay on your payslip, one of these is usually why.

## Reading an offer letter

Two offers with the same CTC can produce noticeably different take-home. Three things drive the gap:

**How much sits in Basic.** Basic is the base for PF, so a higher Basic means a larger PF deduction — lower take-home now, more retirement savings. A lower Basic does the reverse. Neither is wrong, but they are not the same offer.

**How the employer treats the PF ceiling.** Capping the contribution at the ₹15,000 statutory wage ceiling produces higher take-home than contributing on full Basic.

**How much is variable.** A CTC with a large performance-linked component is not guaranteed monthly income.

So ask for the salary structure, not just the CTC. Then work the number forward yourself — the [CTC to in-hand calculator](/resources/ctc-to-in-hand-salary-calculator) does the arithmetic and shows every assumption it makes, so you can change the ones that do not match your offer.

## For employers

The same clarity is worth building into how you communicate salary. Most employee salary queries are not disputes about the amount — they are confusion about which of these four numbers is being discussed. An offer letter that shows the structure, and a payslip that itemises earnings and deductions separately, prevents the conversation entirely.
