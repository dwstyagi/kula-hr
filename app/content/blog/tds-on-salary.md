Of the four statutory deductions on an Indian payslip, three are arithmetic. Provident Fund is a percentage of Basic. ESI is a percentage of gross. Professional Tax is a slab lookup by state.

TDS is not like them. It is an estimate of something that has not happened yet — the employee's tax liability for a financial year that is still running — divided into monthly instalments and revised as the facts firm up. Understanding that one difference explains almost every question employees ask about it.

## What the employer is actually doing

Under Section 192, an employer paying taxable salary must deduct income tax at the time of payment. To know how much, it has to answer a question the employee cannot fully answer in April: what will this person's total tax be for the year?

So the employer builds a projection:

1. **Project annual salary income** — the months already paid, plus the months expected for the rest of the financial year, including known fixed components.
2. **Apply the standard deduction** from salary income. The amount depends on the elected regime and is revised by Finance Acts, so use the figure for the current year rather than one carried forward.
3. **Subtract declared exemptions and deductions** — house rent allowance exemption, and under the old regime the Chapter VI-A deductions the employee has declared.
4. **Apply the slab rates** of the elected regime to the taxable income that remains.
5. **Apply any rebate** the employee qualifies for, then add health and education cess at 4% of the tax.
6. **Divide by the months remaining** in the financial year.

That final division is the step that surprises people. The monthly deduction is *annual liability ÷ remaining months*, so the same annual tax produces a very different monthly figure in April than it does in January.

> Slab rates, the standard deduction and rebate thresholds change with each Finance Act. Any figure you carry forward from last year is a figure worth re-checking — confirm the current year's numbers against the Income Tax Department before you compute.

## The regime election comes first

Nothing else can be computed until you know which regime applies. The **new regime is the default**; an employee who wants the old regime must elect it.

The election is not a formality — it determines which deductions the employer may even consider. Under the old regime, declared investments, house rent, insurance premiums and home-loan interest reduce taxable income. The new regime offers lower slab rates but permits far fewer of those deductions.

Practically, this means your payroll year should start with two collections:

- **The regime election** from every employee
- **The investment declaration** from anyone electing the old regime

Both should be in hand before you compute April's TDS. Every month you wait is a month of deduction based on incomplete facts, and every one of those months has to be corrected later.

## Why TDS moves during the year

Employees experience TDS as unstable, and they are not wrong. It is recalculated whenever the projection changes. The common triggers:

**A salary revision.** A raise in September changes the projected annual income, so the tax changes, so every remaining month changes.

**A declaration that shrinks.** An employee declares ₹1.5 lakh of investments in April and produces proofs for ₹60,000 in February. The gap becomes additional taxable income, and the remaining months absorb the whole correction.

**A declaration submitted late.** The worst version of the same problem. A declaration that arrives in December cannot be spread over a year that has three months left.

**Joining mid-year.** A new employer that does not know about previous-employer salary projects tax only on what it pays. The projection is too low, the deduction is too low, and the employee discovers a liability at filing time. Form 12B is how the employee reports that income so the new employer can compute correctly.

The pattern in all four is the same: TDS did not become wrong: it became *more informed*. The sharpness of the correction is a function of how late the information arrived.

## Getting the year-end right

Two habits remove most of the pain:

**Collect declarations in April, and proofs before the final quarter.** If proofs are due in January rather than March, an employee whose declaration falls short still has two months to absorb the difference instead of one.

**Recompute monthly, not annually.** A projection refreshed every month spreads corrections smoothly. A projection computed once and revisited in February concentrates every correction into the last payrolls of the year, which is what generates the complaints.

## After the deduction

Deducted tax is not the end of the obligation. It becomes a liability the moment it is withheld:

- **Deposit** — generally by the 7th of the following month, with March generally due 30 April for non-government deductors
- **Quarterly returns** — Form 24Q, reporting the deductions made and the employees they relate to
- **Form 16** — issued annually, summarising salary paid and tax deducted, which the employee uses to file their return

Late deposits attract interest and penalties, and TDS that is deducted but not deposited can be disallowed as an expense. Our [payroll compliance calendar](/resources/payroll-compliance-calendar) sets these out month by month with links to the official sources.
